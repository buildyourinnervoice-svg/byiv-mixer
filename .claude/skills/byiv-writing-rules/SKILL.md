# Writing rules

Build Your Inner Voice house style. Every word a member or an employer reads must pass these rules. Apply them to website copy, email bodies inside Make scenarios, PDF sources, quiz answer responses, and to any AI prompt that will itself generate copy (the prompt must carry these rules forward).

## 1. No riddles. This is the rule that keeps getting broken.

A riddle is any sentence that makes the reader do work the writer should have done. Banned shapes, with the fix:

| Banned | Why | Write instead |
|---|---|---|
| "X is not Y. It is Z." | Sets up a contrast for rhythm, not clarity | Just say Z, and say why |
| "That is the whole point." / "That is the thing." | Withholds the point | State the point |
| "...because nothing was ever said to it." | Metaphor the reader has to decode | "...because pushing through does not change what you believe about it." |
| "It works right up until it does not." | Only works as rhythm | "The problem is still there afterwards, and usually slightly bigger." |
| "They need different things said to them." | Opaque | Say what each one needs |
| "That tells you something about who counts." | Makes the reader guess | "...because nobody is waiting on those." |
| "Built for a bad Tuesday." | Cute, and means nothing to a buyer | "Built for the ordinary difficult days when nothing is wrong enough to call anyone about." |
| "...and the morning is gone." | Implied consequence | "...by lunchtime they have been busy for three hours and finished nothing." |
| "Treat this as a lean rather than a type." | Invented jargon dressed as insight | "The top one is only just ahead of the next, so this is a close result rather than a clear one." |
| "The one nobody puts on the intranet." | Oblique as a heading, so the reader cannot tell what the section is | "Support for menopause at work" |
| "Picture someone at eleven in the morning with more to do than the day will hold." | Scene-setting the reader has to decode, and a word number | "A typical example is someone who has more work than they can finish that day." |
| "For the hard days nobody calls a helpline about." | A slogan starting "For the..." that nobody would say out loud, and it frames the product around struggling | Say it the way you'd say it to someone in person, e.g. "Help your team build a more positive mindset at work." |

Test: read the sentence and ask "could a tired person misread this, or have to reread it?" If yes, rewrite. If a sentence sounds clever, that is the signal to rewrite it, not to keep it. Then ask "would anyone actually say this out loud?" If not, rewrite it.

## 2. Write in real sentences. Avoiding riddles is not an excuse to write in fragments.

This is the failure that rule 1 causes if it is applied carelessly. Cutting the cleverness out of a sentence must not leave a stump. A clear sentence is usually longer than a clever one, not shorter.

Banned: one-word sentences, verbless fragments, and lists of items punctuated as if they were sentences.

| Banned | Write instead |
|---|---|
| "Confidence before a room full of senior people. Sleep. Money worry. Something happening well outside work." | "Confidence before a room full of senior people, sleep, money worry, or something happening well outside work altogether." |
| "Sleep works on putting down what is still undone before bed. Money widens attention back out from the shortfall. Confidence pairs belief with a next step." | "Sleep works on putting down whatever is still undone before bed, money widens attention back out from the shortfall, and confidence pairs belief with a next step." |
| "Nothing to attend. Nothing to keep up with. Nobody checking." | "There is nothing to attend, nothing to keep up with, and nobody checking whether they used it." |

Use commas and conjunctions. Three related things belong in one sentence joined by commas and an "and" or an "or", not in three sentences.

Test: read the paragraph aloud. If it sounds like a list being read out, or like someone being terse with you, join the fragments back into sentences. Two or three short sentences in a row is a signal to check; four is always wrong.

A genuinely short sentence is fine when it carries a whole thought ("Minimum of five." in a pricing note). The fault is a run of them.

## 3. Contrast must always be explained

Never state a contrast and stop. "Grief and money worries do not need the same thing said to them" is banned. Say what each one IS built on.

## 4. Plain English

- Short words. Use contractions, the way people speak: can't, don't, isn't, it's, you're. Writing "do not" or "it is" in full sounds stiff.
- Banned: journey, unlock, embrace, lean into, hold space, empower, holistic.
- Do not use internal product words the customer won't know. "A focus that often fits" is wrong, "Focus areas people often choose for mum" is right.
- Do not invent vocabulary and then rely on it. If a word is doing a special job in this product ("steady answer", "a lean", "your type"), either explain it in the same sentence or describe the thing in ordinary words instead. "You picked the answer where it simply did not bother you" beats "you picked the steady answer".
- Jargon must be named in plain words first. Not "an if-then", but "deciding in advance, in the form 'when this happens, I will do that'". Not "implementation intentions". Not "cyclic sighing" without explaining it.
- Numerals, not words: 3 breaths, 5 minutes, 66 days, 12 areas.

## 5. Punctuation

- No em dashes and no en dashes. Use commas, full stops or brackets.
- No semicolons.
- Curly apostrophes are fine in HTML; write them as entities in Make IML where needed.
- Single quote marks for quoted words and thoughts on the page: ‘I’m not good enough’, not “I’m not good enough”. (The double quotes in these rules are only for showing examples.)

## 6. Say what a thing is, not only what it is not

"Not built on the 5 stages" must lead with what it IS built on. The negative can follow.

## 7. Never oversell, and never claim evidence we do not have

- Describe research as what our methods are based on, for example "the research our tracks are built on" or "in the studies our method is based on". That wording already makes it clear we didn't run the studies, so do not add a separate line saying the studies did not test Build Your Inner Voice.
- Give sample sizes and dates when citing a study.
- Never claim BYIV reduces absence, turnover or sickness. It has not been tested for that.
- Never imply it replaces therapy, an EAP, HR or medical care.
- Never tell the reader something about themselves that the data does not support. If a quiz result rests on two answers out of eight, say it is a close result rather than announcing a type.

## 8. Accuracy about privacy, every single time

These are the current facts. If any copy contradicts them, the copy is wrong:

- An employer CAN see which email addresses have claimed a membership, so they can hand memberships out and take them back. Always add: that list is for organising memberships, and is not a record of who has used one.
- An employer CANNOT see whether a named person has used their membership, what anyone wrote, chose or listened to, or which tools any one person used.
- Monthly employer totals only: usage shown from 3 people up, "felt better" from 5 answers up.
- Free text typed into a 5 minute reset, team session or planner is used once and never stored.
- The usage record holds no name, no email and nothing anyone wrote, and is deleted after 100 days.

## 9. Product naming

- Always 5 minute reset in full, never "reset" alone. Prefix with BYIV where it reads naturally: the BYIV 5 minute reset.
- 66 day planner, 30-day challenge, membership page, team session, team code.
- Say the track is written around the goal the person describes, never "in their own words" or "using their words", because it does not quote them.
- Do not put a count in the copy where the library can grow ("24 resets", "12 for managers"). Write "a set written for people who manage others". A number that must be updated in five places is a number that will end up wrong in one of them.

## 10. Headings

- One heading per section. Do not stack a small label above a large heading that says the same thing.
- Every heading must be larger than the body text beneath it. A 12px label above 16px prose reads as a mistake.
- A heading names its subject in plain words. If the section is about menopause, the heading says menopause. Save the good line for the first sentence, where a reader who is already reading will meet it.
- If a section collapses or appears in a contents list, the label there and the heading must be the same words.

## 11. Negativity and blame

Do not blame a third party the reader has to work with. "A manager who keeps adding to it" is banned. Describe the situation, not a villain.

## 12. Emails built in Make

- Every optional box or bullet must be wrapped in an if() so it disappears completely when its field is empty. A heading with an empty body is a bug.
- Never put an escaped double quote inside a {{ }} IML expression. Put literals in a util:SetVariables module and reference those.
- Give boxes room: cell padding around 22px 25px, 28px between boxes, 14px between a box heading and its text, line-height 1.85.

## 13. Open every page with the reason someone would relate to it

The first heading and first sentence of every page name what the reader recognises in themselves, and only then say what Build Your Inner Voice does about it. That can be a worry ("For when the voice in your head is harder on you than anyone else"), but it can equally be a goal: more confidence, a more positive mindset at work, better habits. A page that opens with the product name or a feature list is wrong.

Check every page against the 4 questions the website was designed around:

1. Do I immediately understand what this page is about?
2. Do I understand how the product on this page can help me?
3. Does the page make me want to explore further?
4. Do I feel confident enough to take the next step?

## 14. Keep Sylvia's first-person voice

Where copy is personal to the founder, it stays in the first person ("I", "my"). Never rewrite it as "our founder". Warm, human phrases she uses, such as "a shout out to", stay. Bylines read "Written by Sylvia, the founder of Build Your Inner Voice".

## 15. Lead with the positive, and don't add caveats to claims we aren't making

The membership is for proactive, positive people as well as people having a hard time: people who want a more positive mindset at work, to plan ahead, build better habits and grow in confidence, with a 5 minute reset for the odd bad day. Do not frame everything around struggling.

- Lead with what it does and who it's for, including the ambitious and the doing-fine. Problems can appear, but they are not the whole story.
- If the copy doesn't claim something, don't add a line denying it. Saying people can work on burnout is not a claim to cure or prevent it, so it needs no "it won't fix your workload" or "it isn't treatment" after it. A disclaimer nobody asked for makes the product sound weaker and the writer sound nervous.
- The limits that genuinely matter (not a replacement for therapy or an EAP, not a crisis service) are said once, where they belong: the EAP answer, the "if someone is really struggling" answer, and the terms. Not after every benefit.
- When a topic is included because people search for it (burnout, menopause at work, workplace wellbeing, staff wellbeing), name it plainly as something people can work on.

## Before you finish

Re-read every new sentence against rules 1, 2 and 4, every page opening against rule 13, and the whole piece against rule 15. Rules 1 and 2 fail together: the copy gets de-cleverised into fragments. Check that what you have written still reads like a person talking, and that it doesn't sound more negative than the product is.
